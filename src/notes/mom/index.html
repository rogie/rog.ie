My mom is alive. For more than fifteen years, I have grieved her anyway.

Her name is Ina. She lives with schizophrenia. That diagnosis is part of her story, but it is not the beginning of it, and it is not all of who she is.

My mom has endured more pain than one person should have to carry. She and my dad divorced when I was a year and a half old. Growing up, I heard stories that she was possessed by demons and that she was part of a cult. One day, she was beaten and left for dead in a ditch in Los Angeles.

She recovered. Somehow, her life turned around.

She was my person

I got to know her by spending every other weekend with her. Of everyone in my family, I am probably most like my mom. She is funny and sarcastic. She is an empath. We just got each other.

She encouraged my art when other people taught me to fear it. Her God was different from the God of the cult I grew up in. She did not live in fear, and being with her helped me start drawing again. It felt like reclaiming a part of myself I had abandoned.

She was my best friend. My mentor. The person I wanted to be like.

Then her world changed

Things started happening in her mind. She heard the voices of demons. She was terrified. Her life unraveled at breakneck speed. She divorced her husband and kept running, trying to escape something that followed her everywhere.

She lived with me. She lived with my brother. We had been taught to understand fear and mental illness through the language of demons, so my brother and I both tried to perform an exorcism on her. We did not understand what was happening. We were scared, she was scared, and the only framework we had failed all of us.

She ran away. She missed her own mother’s death and funeral because of the demons she believed were chasing her. It feels like her life stopped in 2010.

Eventually, she was diagnosed with schizophrenia. We pushed to get her professional help, but she did not believe she was sick. She believed she was a prophet. She ran away and has lived as a nomad ever since.

I bottled it up

I took it hard. She was my best friend. My mom. My guide in this world. The pain was too intense, and I was too emotional to know what to do with it, so I did what I had learned to do with pain: I buried it.

On the surface, I became a successful designer. I had influence, a wife, and three kids. Underneath, I was lonely. I had no close friends. I cried most mornings before getting up to work.

Keep it together, Rogie. You are a provider. You are a dad. You cannot break down. Be a man.

But I broke.

There were too many years of pain: the cult of my childhood, the fear of hell, and the loss of my mom even while she was still alive. Bottling it all up had turned into self-hate.

People are your people

I finally went to therapy, a world I had been taught was secular and therefore dangerous. My therapist told me to seek friends in places where I would not expect them. To be vulnerable and real. To share my whole self.

Designers aren’t your people. People are your people.

So I took the damn advice. I opened my heart to anyone who would listen. Something broke free inside me.

Vulnerability saved me. Community saved me. Neither one erased what happened to my mom or brought back the years we lost, but they gave me somewhere to carry the grief. They taught me that pain becomes survivable when we stop carrying it alone.

My mom was a beautiful person. She is a beautiful person.

I am sharing her story because schizophrenia should not erase the person who was there before it, or the person who is still there now. Ina is not a demon, a prophet, a diagnosis, or a tragedy. She is funny. She is sarcastic. She is an empath. She encouraged my art. She helped me become myself.

She is my mom. I love her. I miss her. I am still learning how to hold both.
